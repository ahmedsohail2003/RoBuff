# How RoBuff works

One page, one part per section. Every number below came from a test or a measurement run on this laptop on 2026-11-07 and 2026-11-08. The runs used Windows 11 and headless Chrome, on an RTX 4050 or the Intel Iris Xe where noted. Everything here is a SIMULATION: there is no real robot. Parts that are not built yet say so.

## Hand to arm

The webcam image goes to Google's MediaPipe hand landmarker, which runs in the browser on your own machine and returns 21 points on your hand. From those points the app reads a palm position and a pinch (thumb tip to index tip, divided by the palm size). Moving your hand side to side or up and down in the image moves the gripper the same way. Your right hand moves it to screen right (`?flip=1` is for a camera that mirrors its own picture). How big your palm looks moves it nearer or farther. The hand gain is 1.4, so about 70% of the earlier hand travel covers the same arm range. A One Euro filter, a common smoothing filter for shaky tracking, smooths the palm position. Its beta is 3.0 side to side and up and down, and 1.0 for depth. The target follows with a 0.08 s lag. In the builder's still-hand test, the landmarks had 2.5 px of noise. The target moved 2.77 mm RMS in depth and under 0.5 mm in the other two directions. A 1 cm step reached 90% in 0.38 s (0.48 s before). These control values were tested by their builder, not yet by a separate check. The pinch closes the jaw below 0.25 and opens it above 0.38, only after 2 frames agree. Those thresholds were set from test images and the scripted hand. I have steered the arm with my own hand on a laptop webcam, and it worked. That try was not timed and no numbers were logged. So every measured number here still comes from the scripted, photo and fake-camera tests. The arm then solves damped least squares inverse kinematics on MuJoCo's own Jacobian (reachable test targets landed within 0.09 to 1.85 mm). In a fake-camera test, tracking took a median of 11 ms per frame (maximum 17.5 ms, 120 frames) on the RTX 4050. It found a hand in 123 of 123 frames. When Chrome picked the Intel Iris Xe instead, the median was 26.6 ms. The physics runs at 240 frames per second headless on the RTX 4050, with a median of 0.30 to 0.40 ms of physics per frame.

## Friction grip

Nothing glues the rock to the jaw: no weld, no parenting. The jaw closes on it, and it rises only if contact friction holds it. The arm's actuators and contact values are the public model's own (position servos with gain 50, a 3.5 N m limit, pad friction 1). A scripted pick of the 20 g test cube rose 78.2 mm, stayed held for 5.65 s with 0.054 mm of jaw penetration, and fell 0.25 s after the jaw opened. Five repeats from the same start all passed, which shows the run is deterministic, not that it is robust. Over 9 cube positions, 6 picks passed; the 3 that failed were 8 to 12 cm off the arm's centre line, because the jaw always points along the line from the base.

## Delay and ghost

Light takes 1.28 s each way between the Earth and the Moon (384,400 km, the mean distance in NASA's NSSDC Moon fact sheet, divided by the speed of light: 1.2822 s one way, 2.5644 s round trip). In Moon mode your commands wait 1.28 s before they reach the true arm, and the view of the true arm waits another 1.28 s before it reaches you: in the unit test a command sent at 1.00 s reached the true arm at 2.29 s (1.28 s plus one 10 ms control step). A second simulation, the ghost, runs your commands at once and is drawn as a see-through arm. With identical settings the ghost and the true arm stayed within 0.2 mm of each other over a 10 s scripted pick. The ghost holds a nominal rock (43.2 g: 1.6 g/cm³, friction 0.8) while the true arm holds values the ghost does not know (62.1 g: 2.3 g/cm³, friction 0.6), so the two really disagree: the rock error during the grasp reached 1.19 mm, shown live on the error meter. Two simulations took a median of 2.1 ms of physics per frame or less in the browser tests. A trial logger times each grab and writes one CSV row per trial for the pre-registered ghost test. No ghost result exists yet (0 logged trials).

## Guardrail and lift test

When you ask for an object, a spec (kind, size, material) becomes a generated mesh, and a guardrail checks it in steps before it can appear: the mesh is sane, its convex hull, which way the jaw should face it, its scale, its mass, whether friction can hold it, and the grip height. The limits are measured on the simulated arm: the jaw opens 10 to 70 mm (15 to 55 mm for hand flying), it lifts up to 0.30 kg (200 g for hand flying), and it squeezes with about 50 N. A request that is too big is scaled down and the receipt says by how much; one that cannot work is refused with the numbers. Then a headless lift test runs the same arm on a second copy of the physics state, and only a rise of at least 48 mm at the end of the hold counts as a pass. In the browser test: the Bennu-like sample passed (rise 77.3 mm, held 2.02 s, both jaws touching); "an asteroid the size of Texas" was too wide as asked, so it was rescaled to a 55 mm model of 144 g, which then lifted 76.0 mm; "solid iron, 60 mm" was refused at 562 g; the comet ice shard was refused because the pads meet it 14 degrees off square and it needs friction 0.26 where ice has 0.03. Recompiling the scene with a new object took 211 to 259 ms in Chrome, and the lift test 399 to 551 ms. The specs come from Gemini 3.8 Flash when a key is set (see Voice), else from an offline keyword parser in the browser; a badge on screen says which one answered. Besides the space kinds (asteroid, panel, dish, bus, fragment), Gemini can choose everyday kinds (box, cylinder, sphere, cone) and must say when its shape is a stand-in, which the receipt shows as "Closest shape I can make": in the builder's live check "a soda can" became a 66 by 122 mm cylinder and "a tennis ball" a 67 mm sphere. Either way, the guardrail and the lift test above decide what may spawn.

Recognisable things (a rifle, a plane, a car, a mug) get a third route: an assembly. Gemini is asked for 3 to 8 coloured simple parts at real size, each with a shape, a size, a place and a colour. Each part becomes its own convex hull, the tightest dent-free outline around it, because MuJoCo tests contact quickly only on convex shapes; one hull around the whole object would let the fingers close on empty space. The parts are placed in one body with one collision shape per part, so the model has several shapes but moves as one rigid object. The guardrail runs the same steps on the whole assembly: it scales it down to fit the jaw, makes a thin object thicker when the fingertip pads would not get a grip (the receipt then calls it a toy model), and sums the parts' own hull volumes for the mass, as MuJoCo weighs them, which the receipt states. In the unit tests, for 4 of 4 test assemblies the mass MuJoCo compiled equalled the receipt mass (ratio 1.000000): AK-47 (5 parts, 39.2480 g), car (6, 196.0000 g), plane (4, 13.6024 g) and mug (2, 196.0000 g), and all settled on the floor. The AK-47 then went through the full path, guardrail, settle and headless lift: it was scaled to fit (ADJUSTED) and rose 77.5 mm with the jaw turned to 165 degrees, held 2.07 s with both jaws touching. In one final live Gemini run (2026-11-08 00:23), 5 of 5 requests were scaled to fit and lifted: AK-47, 7 parts, 149.8 x 38.4 x 9.4 mm, 42.53 g, rose 76.3 mm; a plane, 7 parts, 66.1 x 64.3 x 22.7 mm, 10.45 g, 78.3 mm; a Tesla, 6 parts, 106.2 x 43.8 x 27.1 mm, 196 g, 75.8 mm; a coffee mug, 5 parts, 52.9 x 41.7 x 48.3 mm, 196 g, 69.2 mm; Minecraft grass block, 5 parts, 40.3 x 39.9 x 39.4 mm, 196 g, 74.3 mm. Earlier live runs at 23:48 and 00:10 also lifted 5 of 5, with different part counts and sizes, because Gemini writes a new spec each time. These are toy-size models built from simple parts, not detailed copies. Two limits: where parts overlap, their shared volume is counted twice in the mass, and the parts and sizes change between Gemini runs.

## Skins

A skin changes how an object looks, never how it behaves. You type a look in the skin box under the receipt, pick a preset, upload an image, or say "paint it gold". The key server is a small server on your own machine that holds the API keys. With a Gemini key there, Nano Banana, Google's Gemini image models, draws the picture. Its pictures are AI-made and carry SynthID, Google's invisible mark for AI images. High quality uses `gemini-3-pro-image`, shown as Nano Banana Pro, and is the default. Fast uses `gemini-3.1-flash-lite-image`, shown as Nano Banana 2 Lite. Only the description is sent, inside a fixed prompt that asks for a flat texture that tiles with no visible edges. Without a key, or when a call fails, the browser draws a seeded pattern instead, tinted by colour words. The recipes are carbon fibre, camouflage, galaxy, lava, marble, wood, checker, stripes, rust, gold, chrome, brushed metal and plain noise. The same text always gives the same pattern. An uploaded PNG, JPEG or WebP is cropped to a square in the browser and never sent anywhere. Every texture is 1024x1024. The hulls have no texture coordinates, the map that says where each point of a picture lands on a shape. So the picture is projected from the sides of a box fitted to the object. It wraps every part, and part words such as "wheels" pick parts. The first material word sets the finish. Gold or chrome looks like shiny metal, and rust, wood, galaxy or nebula stay dull. The skin goes on the drawn mesh only. Hulls, mass, friction and the MuJoCo model never change, and the status line says so. A badge says where each skin came from, for example "skin: Nano Banana Pro (cached)", "skin: Nano Banana 2 Lite (live)" or "skin: procedural (offline)".

The key server keeps each picture in a disk cache in `web/.skin-cache`, which git ignores. The cache key is the model and the cleaned-up text. It also caps pictures at 100 units a day, with 1 unit for Fast and 4 for High quality. That is 100 Fast or 25 High quality pictures. A cache hit uses none of the cap. The "(cached)" badge and the finish rule passed a separate re-check at 04:59 EST. The cap and the server's text clean-up passed the key server tests in an independent fresh-copy test of `c40ec1e`. In the 04:09 re-check, a fresh Fast picture took 3515 ms on the key server. A disk cache hit took 39.8 to 207.7 ms in the browser. The browser also keeps the last 8 skins, and a repeat from there takes 0 ms. In the unit test, in Node, the fastest of 3 draws of a procedural pattern took 31 to 78 ms of CPU time. The galaxy Tesla got all 6 parts painted and still rose 76 mm when picked. Builder-tested only: fresh Fast pictures took 3.1 to 4.5 s over six draws, and High quality took 12.1 to 12.9 s over three. The fallback after a failed call was tested only against a stand-in server that fakes the failure. The picture is projected from a box, so it can show seams on round parts. How often was not measured.

## Gravity

The gravity menu writes a new value into every running simulation at once: Earth 9.82, Mars 3.73 and Moon 1.62 m/s² (NASA NSSDC fact sheets), Bennu 8.3e-5 m/s² (calculated from JPL's GM 4.8904 m³/s² and diameter 484.44 m, spin ignored), and orbit 0. A cube released 0.10 m above the floor touched it after 0.354 s on the Moon (theory 0.351 s) and 0.144 s on Earth (theory 0.143 s); on Bennu it was still 0.09896 m up after 5 s. On Bennu the screen shows the escape speed, about 0.20 m/s, and says "That rock is gone." once the rock is more than 1.5 m from the pad and moving faster than that. The sim's gravity is uniform, so this is a label, not a simulated escape. Contact at very low gravity is not trustworthy: a 5 cm/s sideways push lifted the resting cube 46.9 mm on Bennu, against 0.044 mm on the Moon and none on Earth, so the receipt carries a warning whenever gravity is below 0.1 m/s².

## Voice

Hold V (or the Hold to talk button) and speak, or type in the box. A fixed offline grammar handles commands (stop, resume, home, open, close, the five gravities, delay and ghost on or off, make or give me an object) and never waits for the network; its tests map 20 phrases to the right action and reject 3 nonsense phrases. Object requests go to Gemini 3.8 Flash through a small local key server when a key is set; in the live check on 2026-11-07, 5 of 5 requests came back as valid specs, median 3.9 s end to end (3.1 to 5.4 s). Without a key, an offline keyword mock answers. Spoken replies use ElevenLabs (`eleven_flash_v2_5`) through the same key server when its key is set: in the live check on 2026-11-07, 5 of 5 lines returned audio with no errors, first audio median 176 ms (167 to 445 ms). Without that key the browser's own voice speaks, labelled offline. Captions always. Badges on screen say which source answered and which voice spoke. Voice can never clear a stop.

Talk live is the hands-free version. One press starts Chrome's speech recognition, which turns speech into text. It restarts itself when a listening session ends. It pauses while RoBuff speaks, so it does not hear its own voice. A second press turns it off. Browsers without speech recognition show "Talk live (not in this browser)" and a note to type instead. Each line goes to the offline grammar first. Once Chrome's speech recognition has turned "stop" into text, it is always handled there, with no chat call. The spoken line is "Stopped. The arm holds where it is. Press Resume on screen to go on." A line the grammar does not know goes to a `/api/chat` route on the key server. It carries the recent turns and the app's state. The state is the mode, gravity, last object and whether the arm is stopped. It also has the object's name and part names, whether the arm holds it, and the gripper position. Gemini 3.8 Flash answers with a reply and up to 3 actions. The browser checks the answer again. It cuts the reply to 200 characters, and an action that is not on the fixed list becomes none. A spawn goes through the same guardrail and lift test as a typed request. When the guardrail refuses an object for weight and names a lighter size, Talk live asks once for that size. One live run on 2026-11-08 used the real key server and a fake microphone. A question about Moon gravity got a reply in 2850 ms in the browser (2841 ms at Gemini). The reply quoted 1.62 m/s², and the first ElevenLabs audio played 530 ms after it. "Make me a tennis ball" was refused at 67 mm (234 g, over the 200 g limit). The follow-up at 52 mm passed at 195.9 g. "Switch to Mars" and "stop" went to the local grammar with 0 chat calls. After "stop", the arm's commands did not change over 0.5 simulated s. In an earlier live run the spoken replies to those two lines started after 183 ms and 473 ms. Another run in the same round failed its check because the lift test dropped the follow-up ball at one random seed. It is reported as is. While RoBuff is speaking, Talk live does not listen, so a spoken "stop" waits for the line to end. The Space key, the stop gesture and the typed command still stop the arm at once.

Voice can also move the arm. I added this on 2026-11-08. A line such as "move left 5 cm" goes through the same offline grammar, which reads a direction and a distance. "A bit" is 2 cm, no amount is 5 cm and "a lot" is 10 cm. A move over 30 cm or under half a centimetre is refused. The move becomes a gripper target on the same input path the hand and the mouse use. So the stop latch, the reach clamp, the Moon delay and the ghost apply to voice moves too. The reach clamp can cut a move short. If it cuts more than 2 mm, RoBuff says "That is as far as I can reach" and how far it moved. Each move follows a smooth path that starts and ends gently, at a peak of 0.10 m/s, and lasts at least 0.5 s. A pick uses the lift test's grasp. It counts as holding only when MuJoCo shows the object rose at least 48 mm with both jaws touching it. A line the grammar does not know, such as "pick it up and move it left", goes to Gemini 3.8 Flash when it is live. Its actions then run in order through the same handlers. Stop brakes the arm at once. Queued moves are dropped, and each arm servo is told to hold the joint angle measured at the stop, with a correction for gravity. The jaw keeps its command, so a held object stays held. Voice can set a stop but never clear it. Gemini can neither stop nor resume the arm. Only the Resume button on screen clears a stop. In the Moon modes, voice moves, grip and home are delayed and ghosted like the hand. Picks and put-downs are refused there. In the unit test a 5 cm move left moved the gripper 49.74 mm in 0.94 s. After a stop it moved at most 0.99 mm in 0.5 s. In the verifier's browser test the ghost was on. After a voice move, the ghost moved 2 mm at +0.43 s and the true arm at +1.69 s. Every measured run fed typed transcripts into a fake speech recognizer, not a real microphone.

## Safety

Space stops the arm. The stop latch then sends no new commands, so every joint and the jaw hold where they are and a held rock is not dropped; only the on-screen Resume button clears it. In the browser check, after Space the arm's commands did not change for 2.2 s of simulated time while the physics kept running. If the hand is lost, the arm holds the last palm position and grip; a returning hand must be seen for 2 frames, and the arm target cannot move faster than 0.25 m/s. The stop gesture is both hands open with fingers spread, held 0.4 s (it needs `?hands=2`): in tests it latched at 0.40 s, and a 601-frame pinch-and-carry replay never set it off. A real-hand try of the two-hands gesture has not been logged.

## Prepared answers

### Is this new work? Haven't you built robot arms before?

Yes, I have built related projects: the WorkCell series, EmberHacks "The cell" (submitted to another event) and HandSignal. None of their code is here. Every line of application code in this repo was written during UB Hacking 2026, and the commit history starts after hacking opened. What carries over is what I learned, plus public libraries and the same public arm model (MuJoCo Menagerie's SO-ARM100), all listed in [THIRD_PARTY.md](THIRD_PARTY.md). EmberHacks was a robot cell you instruct by voice, with no physics. RoBuff is your own hand reaching an arm through contact physics, light delay and low gravity. EmberHacks also had a hold-to-talk voice assistant on Gemini Live, Google's live voice API. Through tool calls it ran robot tasks and created and decorated objects. It also had a typed assistant on Gemini Flash. It used a Gemini Flash-lite image model to print generated labels on part faces. None of that code is here either. RoBuff's Fast skins also use a Flash-lite image model, and its chat replies also use Gemini Flash. RoBuff's voice motion and skins are new code, written during this hackathon, and they work differently. A voice move enters the same control path as the hand. So the stop, the reach limit, the Moon delay and the ghost apply to it. A skin can cover every part of an object built from parts, instead of a label printed on part faces.

### Your ghost is a simulation predicting a simulation. Of course it matches.

Right, and that is the biggest limit. If the ghost and the true arm shared one model the ghost would be exact, so the true arm holds rock mass and friction the ghost does not know, and the error between them is shown. That matches the literature: predictive displays have helped with reaching since the 1980s, and they break down at contact. We test one operator, in simulation only, and claim nothing about hardware.

### Real lunar arms will be autonomous. Why hand control?

Mostly they will. The Canadian Space Agency says Canadarm3 will do some tasks on its own, with people operating it when required. This is about the moments when people step in, and it is mainly a teaching tool: you cannot explain light delay as well as you can make someone feel it.

### What does the AI actually do?

A hand-pose model (MediaPipe's hand landmarker) reads your hand on your own machine. Without it there is no hand control. The guardrail is plain rules and a simulated lift test, not AI. Object requests go to Gemini 3.8 Flash when a key is set. Its live check gave 5 of 5 valid specs, median 3.9 s. Gemini only proposes, and code decides what may spawn. Gemini also writes the part lists for objects built from parts and the Talk live replies. A reply's actions, up to 3, come from a fixed list. The browser checks them again. Nano Banana, Google's Gemini image models, draws skins when a key is set. A skin changes only the look. Without a key, the offline keyword mock answers, and the screen says so. The physics and the ghost are not AI.

### How accurate is the physics? Can the arm really lift that?

Gravity values come from NASA and JPL data and the rock densities from published sample work, all listed in [THIRD_PARTY.md](THIRD_PARTY.md). Contact is not validated against anything real: at Bennu gravity MuJoCo shows artefacts (a 5 cm/s push lifted a cube 46.9 mm), regolith is not modelled, and Bennu's spin is ignored. The lift test uses the same simulated arm as the live scene, so "it can lift this" means the simulated arm can, not a real SO-ARM100.
